<h2>Understanding the Reviewer's Perspective</h2>
<p>Before opening a post-processor or writing a single paragraph, it helps to understand what a reviewer is actually looking for. Whether you are submitting a university assignment, a conference paper, or an industrial design review, the evaluator wants to see that you treated the simulation as a rigorous engineering tool, not a "black box" that spits out a pretty picture. They will check three things: <strong>was the physics set up correctly?</strong>, <strong>is the results data trustworthy?</strong>, and <strong>can the reasoning be followed without having to guess?</strong>. If any of those are unclear, the review will either be sent back for revisions or, worse, the conclusions will be dismissed outright. Documenting your CFD work is essentially the same as documenting any other engineering calculation: you show your work, you justify your assumptions, and you verify the outcome.</p>

<h2>Step 1: Document the Problem Definition Before Touching the Solver</h2>
<p>The most effective documentation starts before you even launch ANSYS Fluent or CFX. Write down the physical problem in plain language. What geometry are you analyzing? What is the flow regime (incompressible, compressible, laminar, turbulent)? What are the inlet, outlet, and boundary conditions? This preliminary written description becomes the "north star" for your entire documentation. When you later describe your setup, you can reference these baseline parameters instead of hunting through menus. It also helps you spot missing or inconsistent data early. A common mistake is to define the problem on the fly, which leads to gaps in the documentation that reviewers will flag immediately.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Capture the Mesh Strategy</h2>
<p>The mesh is often the first place reviewers poke for credibility. You need to document not just that a mesh was generated, but <em>how</em> it was generated and why it is adequate. Start by recording the mesh type (structured, unstructured, polyhedral) and the overall element count. Then describe the refinement zones. Did you use a fine mesh near the wall to capture the boundary layer? Did you apply an inflation layer? What was the target y+ range, and how did you justify it? If you performed a mesh independence study, document the sequence of mesh sizes you tested, the resulting changes in key output variables (like lift or drag coefficient), and the point at which you declared convergence. Reviewers want to see that the solution is not mesh-dependent, and a concise table or paragraph showing convergence trends goes a long way toward proving that.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Physical Models and Solver Settings</h2>
<p>This section of the documentation should be a mini-technical note. List every model you activated: gravity, energy equation, species transport, turbulence model (k-epsilon, k-omega SST, Reynolds Stress Model, etc.), and any user-defined functions or custom drag coefficients. For each model, briefly state the default or selected options and the rationale. For example, if you chose the SST k-omega model over standard k-epsilon, note that you did so because of the adverse pressure gradient on the geometry. Document the discretization schemes you used for pressure and momentum (first-order upwind, second-order upwind, or bounded). Mention under-relaxation factors if they were tweaked from defaults, and why. This level of detail tells the reviewer that you understood the numerical implications of your choices, not just that you clicked the right buttons.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Initial Conditions and Solution Initialization</h2>
<p>Often overlooked, the initialization step can affect convergence behavior and final results, especially for steady-state simulations initialized from a previous case or from a coarser mesh. Document the initial field values you set for pressure, velocity, temperature, and any scalars. If you initialized from a prior simulation or from a uniform field, note that. For transient simulations, document the time step size, the total number of physical time steps, and the CFL number target. If you used a coupled or segregated solver, record that choice and the reasoning. A sentence or two here prevents the reviewer from wondering why your solution might be sensitive to the starting state.</p>

<h2>Step 5: Convergence Monitoring and Criteria</h>
<p>Convergence is one of the most misunderstood aspects of CFD documentation. Simply stating "the residuals dropped below 1e-5" is not enough. You need to document <em>what</em> you monitored and <em>how</em> you knew the solution had physically converged. Track the residual history for all equations, but also monitor integral quantities like lift, drag, and forces over time. Explain your convergence criteria: was it based on residual reduction, on the stability of force coefficients, or on a combination of both? For transient cases, discuss periodicity—how many time steps were simulated after the solution reached a periodic state? Providing a simple line graph of residuals versus iteration count, even if hand-drawn in the report, shows the reviewer that you watched the solver behavior and did not just walk away after the green checkmark appeared.</p>

<h2>Step 6: Post-Processing and Result Extraction</h2>
<p>Once the simulation ran, the documentation shifts to what the data actually says. Start by listing the quantities you extracted and the locations or planes where they were computed. If you computed pressure coefficients, skin friction, or velocity profiles, note the reference quantities used (freestream velocity, reference area, fluid density). Describe the visualizations you created: color contours, vectors, streamlines. More importantly, explain what the viewer should look for. Instead of just saying "Figure 5 shows the velocity contour," write "Figure 5 shows the velocity contour normalized by freestream velocity; the recirculation zone behind the cylinder is evident by the low-velocity region." This guides the reviewer's eye and demonstrates that you are interpreting the physics, not just displaying pretty pictures.</p>

<h2>Step 7: Validation and Uncertainty Quantification</h2>
<p>This is the section that often separates a passing submission from an outstanding one. If you have experimental data for comparison, document the comparison quantitatively. Plot the CFD results alongside the experimental measurements, and calculate the error or percentage difference. Mention the conditions under which the comparison was made (same Reynolds number, same geometry, same boundary conditions). If you do not have experimental data, discuss the expected sources of error: mesh resolution, turbulence model selection, boundary condition approximations, and geometric simplifications. Acknowledging these limitations and explaining their likely impact shows maturity as a practitioner. Reviewers respect a simulation that honestly discusses its own uncertainty rather than one that pretends to be perfectly accurate.</p>

<h2>Step 8: Final Review Checklist</h2>
<p>Before hitting submit, run through a quick documentation audit. Does every parameter used in the simulation appear somewhere in the write-up? Are the units consistent throughout? Does the flow of the documentation match the order of the simulation steps? Are all figures and tables labeled with clear captions that stand alone without referring back to the text? A common pitfall is assuming the reader remembers what was defined three sections ago—explicit labeling eliminates that friction. If possible, have a peer read the documentation with fresh eyes; if they can reconstruct the setup and results solely from what you wrote, you have succeeded.</p>

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Documenting a CFD simulation is not a bureaucratic afterthought; it is an integral part of the engineering process. A well-documented case communicates that you treated the simulation with the same rigor as a hand calculation or a wind tunnel test. By systematically recording the problem definition, mesh strategy, physical models, initialization, convergence behavior, result extraction, and validation, you give the reviewer a clear path to approve your work. The effort you put into the documentation is directly reflected in the speed and positivity of the review outcome. Treat your simulation case file with the same care you would a design drawing, and the approvals will follow.</p>
